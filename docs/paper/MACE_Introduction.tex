\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Predicting the transient response of a physical system to prescribed control actions is an important part of system analysis and design. In energy systems these actions affect coupled processes whose characteristic time scales may differ substantially. Changes in reactor power can precede changes in component temperatures while thermal feedback influences the subsequent neutronic response. Physics-based models describe these relationships through governing equations and provide simulated responses for operating conditions that may be difficult to investigate experimentally. Repeated simulation of many possible control trajectories can nevertheless become computationally demanding. Data-driven surrogates offer a means of approximating these responses, provided that their training data represent the relevant dynamics and their forecasts remain accurate beyond the observed history.

For transient surrogate development the input is a control trajectory rather than an isolated control value. The corresponding multivariate response trajectory depends on the sequence, rate, and duration of preceding actions. Two trajectories can reach the same actuator position with different physical states and different subsequent responses. Training data must therefore provide meaningful dynamical excitation while retaining the temporal relationships between control and response. Forecasting introduces a second requirement. A recurrent model trained to predict from reference histories must subsequently advance using its own predictions when future responses are unavailable. Prediction errors can alter the histories encountered during this recurrence and propagate between coupled output variables. Accurate single-step regression must consequently be distinguished from accurate generation of a complete forecast trajectory.

Uncertainty quantification introduces a third requirement because forecasting accuracy alone does not determine the reliability of prediction intervals. Independently trained ensemble members can supply forecast-dependent disagreement, but this spread requires calibration against reference responses before it can support a specified coverage objective. The objective itself must distinguish coverage at one forecast coordinate from simultaneous coverage across time and output variables. Its interpretation also depends on the relationship between the calibration distribution and the evaluation distribution. This study develops the Multivariate Adaptive Conformal Ensemble (MACE) framework around these connected requirements. Temporally structured stochastic controls support an autoregressive LSTM ensemble whose spread supplies an adaptive scale for conformal calibration. We investigate different coverage objectives and compare calibration transfer with independent batch recalibration while retaining the same forecasting models. Operation-informed heat-pipe microreactor transients provide the application test of this general forecasting and uncertainty-quantification framework.

\subsection{Literature review}
\label{sec:intro:literature}

The selection of simulation data determines which physical behaviors a surrogate can learn directly and which it must infer from limited examples. Active learning addresses this problem by allocating additional simulations according to information supplied by an evolving model. Pestourie \emph{et al.}\ used active acquisition to develop deep surrogates for partial differential equations in metasurface design~\cite{pestourie2020surrogates}. Kapadia \emph{et al.}\ coupled a non-intrusive error estimator with parameter-specific proper orthogonal decomposition and kernel-based surrogate learning~\cite{kapadia2024surrogate}. These methods concentrate simulation effort within a parameter space, but learning a controlled dynamical system additionally requires inputs that reveal its response over time. Hjalmarsson linked experiment design to identification accuracy and intended model use, emphasizing the interaction between experimental constraints and performance requirements~\cite{hjalmarsson2005experiment}. Schoukens and Ljung developed this perspective for nonlinear identification, where excitation and operating conditions affect the dynamics that can be inferred from a dataset~\cite{schoukens2019roadmap}. Accordingly, covering actuator amplitudes alone does not establish informative coverage of transient behavior. Motion rates, reversals, and holding periods influence the states visited and the relationships available to a sequence model. Sampling complete control trajectories makes these relationships explicit, although a prescribed sampling distribution is not itself a proof of identifiability or an optimal experiment design. The relevant question is whether the resulting response trajectories support accurate forecasts under the conditions subsequently evaluated.

Temporally structured excitation can be specified through deterministic maneuvers or stochastic processes with controlled correlation. Zimmer \emph{et al.}\ parameterized input trajectories as consecutive sections and selected them sequentially using Gaussian-process models of information gain and safety~\cite{zimmer2018safe}. Their formulation connects temporal input design to learning a dynamical response while distinguishing admissibility from informativeness. Gaussian-process covariance models also provide a practical basis for prescribing a trajectory population before model training. The Mat\'ern family relates covariance parameters to process regularity and correlation length~\cite{porcu2024matern}. Applied to control velocity, it specifies correlated motion whose integration produces an angular trajectory with a defined initial position. Correlation length governs the persistence of motion while velocity variance influences displacement dispersion. Initialization and rejection of inadmissible angles further shape the accepted population, so the covariance of the unconditioned velocity process and the distribution of retained controls must be distinguished. In this study all original stochastic trajectories were sampled using a common velocity semivariogram. The verified retained trajectories satisfy an angular-velocity magnitude below $1^\circ\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$. This is a property of the retained dataset, whereas the Gaussian covariance does not impose a hard velocity bound. The sampling procedure is prescribed before training and does not select new simulations using surrogate uncertainty. Its role is to establish reproducible temporal excitation for the forecasting study.

Sequence models provide a means of learning the relationship between this excitation and the resulting response. Long short-term memory networks introduce gated recurrent dynamics that support learning dependencies across an input sequence~\cite{hochreiter1997lstm}. In nuclear applications Radaideh \emph{et al.}\ investigated neural forecasting of loss-of-coolant accident transients, while Appiah \emph{et al.}\ used LSTM system identification within model predictive control of sodium cold-trap purification temperature~\cite{radaideh2020loca,appiah2024coldtrap}. These applications establish the relevance of recurrent representations to coupled physical processes, but the forecast task depends on both the information supplied to the model and its deployment strategy. Prediction from an observed history, direct prediction of a future sequence, and recursive advancement of a single-step model impose different demands. In the recursive case each predicted response contributes to subsequent inputs while the prescribed future control remains available. The network must reproduce the evolution implied by that control through histories that increasingly contain its own approximation errors. For multivariate responses this entails more than independently fitting several output curves. Histories of coupled quantities jointly influence the next prediction, and the relevant forecasting assessment must retain their temporal evolution. Nuclear sequence-model applications therefore motivate the architecture without establishing the recursive accuracy or interval coverage of a new surrogate. These properties require evaluation using the same forecast procedure employed during inference.

The difference between training and recursive inference is a recognized limitation of recurrent sequence prediction. Bengio \emph{et al.}\ showed how replacing reference inputs with generated outputs creates a discrepancy that can produce accumulating errors, motivating scheduled sampling during training~\cite{bengio2015scheduled}. In physical forecasting Vlachas \emph{et al.}\ investigated LSTM prediction of high-dimensional chaotic systems in reduced coordinates and distinguished short-term forecasting from longer-term dynamical behavior~\cite{vlachas2018chaotic}. Together these studies illustrate why a small one-step loss is insufficient to establish sustained forecasting performance. An initial error can change the next input history, which then changes subsequent errors through the learned recurrence. Depending on the dynamics and local model sensitivities this perturbation can grow, decay, or shift the timing of a transient. Coupling introduces additional paths for propagation between output variables. For the controlled systems considered here the forecast must also reproduce thermal settling after actuator motion ends, rather than only the immediate response during motion. We therefore evaluate complete recursive forecast trajectories independently of one-step training performance. Each ensemble member advances using its own preceding predictions and the prescribed control trajectory. Predictions are converted from output-scaled coordinates to the input-scaled history before feedback, preserving the intended recurrence. This conversion is necessary for consistent deployment, while the scientific question concerns the accuracy of the resulting multivariate evolution.

Deep ensembles offer a practical way to associate such forecasts with model-dependent variability. Lakshminarayanan \emph{et al.}\ demonstrated predictive uncertainty estimation using independently trained neural networks and probabilistic regression outputs~\cite{lakshminarayanan2017deep}. For deterministic recurrent members, differences in training subsets and fitted parameters instead produce distinct forecast trajectories whose population standard deviation measures disagreement. This variability can respond to the control trajectory and change across forecast horizons, making it a candidate scale for adaptive intervals. However, members can share a systematic forecasting error even when their predictions agree. Their spread therefore cannot be assumed to identify all epistemic or aleatoric uncertainty or to define a calibrated predictive distribution. Kuleshov \emph{et al.}\ addressed regression calibration by recalibrating predicted cumulative probabilities~\cite{kuleshov2018calibrated}. Levi \emph{et al.}\ further showed that aggregate regression-calibration criteria can fail to distinguish informative uncertainty estimates from uninformative ones~\cite{levi2022regression}. Ovadia \emph{et al.}\ demonstrated deteriorating uncertainty behavior under dataset shift in classification benchmarks~\cite{ovadia2019shift}. Although these studies address different prediction settings, they collectively separate obtaining an uncertainty estimate from evaluating its statistical usefulness. For transient regression this requires considering forecasting error, empirical coverage, and interval sharpness separately. Narrow intervals are useful only in relation to their coverage objective, and a calibrated population coverage rate does not establish that the ensemble scale tracks difficulty for every operating condition.

Conformal prediction separates interval calibration from the construction of the underlying predictor. In split conformal regression a model is fitted on a training partition and a held-out calibration partition supplies nonconformity scores. A finite-sample order statistic of these scores determines the prediction-set threshold. Under exchangeability of calibration and evaluation samples, conditional on the fitted predictor and independently established score rule, the rank argument supplies marginal coverage for a new sample~\cite{lei2018regression,angelopoulos2023conformal}. Absolute-error scores give symmetric intervals whose width is constant across new inputs for a fixed calibrated threshold. Normalized scores instead divide the error by a positive input-dependent scale, allowing interval width to vary with estimated prediction difficulty. Papadopoulos \emph{et al.}\ developed normalized regression nonconformity measures for this purpose~\cite{papadopoulos2008normalized}. Romano \emph{et al.}\ introduced conformalized quantile regression, which calibrates learned lower and upper conditional quantiles and can accommodate asymmetric variability~\cite{romano2019cqr}. These approaches share an empirical calibration step but allocate interval width differently. MACE uses the ensemble mean as its center and absolute forecast error divided by ensemble spread plus a common positive floor as its score. The floor maintains a nonzero scale when members agree, while the calibrated multiplier determines the interval width. This construction produces symmetric forecast-dependent intervals without requiring quantile-network training or treating ensemble spread as a Gaussian predictive standard deviation. Its usefulness must be assessed against absolute-error conformal intervals under matched calibration data and coverage objectives.

Extending this construction from individual coordinates to an entire multivariate response requires defining the calibration unit and the event to be covered. Diquigiovanni \emph{et al.}\ treated each multivariate functional response as one statistical object and constructed simultaneous prediction bands around an arbitrary regression estimator~\cite{diquigiovanni2022multivariate}. Their score takes the supremum of absolute residuals normalized by positive modulation functions over both the component functions and their domains. A quantile across exchangeable regression pairs then defines a common multiplier for the component bands. This permits dependence within a functional response while targeting its simultaneous inclusion in the band. Their training-derived modulation functions vary with component and domain location. The related functional-band analysis of Diquigiovanni \emph{et al.}\ develops closed-form bands with finite-sample validity and addresses their efficiency~\cite{diquigiovanni2025bands}. These formulations provide a direct statistical foundation for trajectory-wide coverage. MACE's joint construction uses the same maximum-score principle on a discrete forecast grid, but its scale varies between control-conditioned forecasts through ensemble disagreement. MACE-Horizon calibrates each horizon and output variable separately, whereas MACE-Trajectory calibrates each output's maximum normalized error over the trajectory. The former does not automatically guarantee Horizon complete multivariate coverage, and the latter does not automatically guarantee Trajectory complete multivariate coverage. Joint trajectory calibration takes the maximum over all horizons and outputs within each calibration trajectory before selecting one quantile across trajectories. Its simultaneous objective concerns the complete recorded multivariate response trajectory, rather than every continuous instant. Temporal and multivariate dependence within a trajectory is retained without promoting its individual coordinates to independent calibration samples.

Other conformal forecasting approaches address different combinations of sequential dependence and simultaneous coverage. Xu and Xie's EnbPI combines bootstrap ensemble predictions with residual-based intervals and bounds coverage gaps under assumptions on prediction quality and the stochastic errors of a time series~\cite{xu2021enbpi}. Its sequential residual updates differ from split calibration using separate simulated trajectories. Stankevi\v{c}i\={u}t\.{e} \emph{et al.}\ calibrate multi-horizon forecasts from exchangeable sequences and use multiplicity correction to obtain joint coverage~\cite{stankeviciute2021forecasting}. Schlembach \emph{et al.}\ extend this strategy to non-exchangeable multi-target conformal prediction~\cite{schlembach2025multivariate}. They form examples through temporal windows, calibrate weighted absolute residuals separately for each horizon and variable, and apply a Bonferroni correction across forecast coordinates. The resulting region is a hyperrectangle. Their miscoverage bound comprises the nominal level and coordinate-specific coverage-gap terms associated with non-exchangeability, whose magnitude depends on the residual weights and distributional differences. Thus their guarantee quantifies departures from exchangeability rather than establishing exact nominal coverage under arbitrary change. MACE also produces rectangular bands, but joint calibration uses one trajectory maximum instead of allocating miscoverage separately across coordinates. This comparison distinguishes coverage construction from region shape. Sun and Yu's CopulaCPTS uses a copula to represent dependence among multi-step forecast errors from independent sequences~\cite{sun2024copulacpts}. Xu, Jiang, and Xie's MultiDimSPCI instead builds ellipsoidal regions for multivariate time-series responses and studies conditional coverage gaps~\cite{xu2024ellipsoidal}. Dependence-aware geometry can represent relationships that rectangular intervals omit. Maximum-score bands retain a simpler coordinate-wise interpretation while accounting for the observed joint residual event through calibration, without explicitly estimating a copula or covariance-shaped region.

Physical-surrogate conformalization provides a further close precedent. Gopakumar \emph{et al.}\ calibrated high-dimensional spatio-temporal surrogate predictions using absolute residual, conformalized quantile, and standard-deviation-normalized scores~\cite{gopakumar2026surrogates}. Their framework preserves the output tensor and computes calibration quantiles separately at each cell, providing marginal coverage at specified locations, lead times, and variables under the required calibration--evaluation exchangeability. Independent simulation input--solution pairs supply calibration samples in their numerical studies, so temporal dependence within a simulated response is compatible with the calibration design. Their standard-deviation score already establishes uncertainty-scale normalization for physical surrogates. Its use of probabilistic model outputs differs from the population spread of MACE's deterministic autoregressive members, and cell-wise coverage differs from a simultaneous trajectory event. Their evaluation also distinguishes new physics regimes relative to training from distributional differences between calibration and evaluation. Moya \emph{et al.}\ similarly conformalized Bayesian and probabilistic DeepONets and developed a quantile DeepONet for differential-equation surrogates~\cite{moya2025deeponet}. These studies demonstrate that physical surrogate uncertainty can be calibrated without identifying the calibration procedure with the predictor architecture. The remaining question for the present work is how an ensemble disagreement scale behaves during control-conditioned recursive forecasting and how alternative score reductions distribute width across coupled transients. Comparing marginal and trajectory objectives on common forecasts exposes this tradeoff while retaining the distinction between surrogate approximation error and uncertainty in the underlying physical model. The reference responses here are generated by a fixed simulator, so the intervals quantify forecasting error relative to that simulator.

Calibration under distribution change requires a separate consideration of which statistical relationship has changed and which data are available. Tibshirani \emph{et al.}\ developed weighted conformal prediction under covariate shift, where the input distribution changes while the conditional response law remains unchanged, using known or estimated likelihood ratios~\cite{tibshirani2019shift}. This approach reweights existing calibration information and requires suitable overlap between input populations. Barber \emph{et al.}\ instead analyzed coverage degradation beyond exchangeability through weighted constructions and bounds involving distributional differences~\cite{barber2023exchangeability}. Online methods use newly revealed responses to adapt continuously. Gibbs and Cand\`es introduced adaptive conformal inference with a long-run coverage-frequency objective under changing distributions, then developed locally adaptive online inference with regret control over time intervals~\cite{gibbs2021adaptive,gibbs2024online}. Xu and Xie's sequential predictive conformal inference estimates future residual quantiles from their temporal dependence and establishes asymptotic conditional coverage under additional assumptions~\cite{xu2023spci}. These methods address distinct information settings and guarantee types. In the present offline study a new simulator-generated calibration dataset can be drawn independently from a prescribed evaluation population. Batch recalibration therefore allows a direct comparison on identical forecasts without estimating control-trajectory likelihood ratios or introducing sequential feedback. Stochastic and operation-informed controls define different trajectory distributions, making unchanged stochastic calibration a distribution-transfer diagnostic. Independent operation-informed calibration and evaluation samples provide a matched-population comparison using the same ensemble, scaling transformations, and interval floor. Recalibration changes thresholds and intervals while leaving mean forecasts unchanged. The corresponding split-conformal claim depends on exchangeability of the trajectory samples and is marginal over their prescribed population. It does not establish conditional coverage for each maneuver type or guarantee the empirical coverage realized by every finite evaluation batch. In MACE, adaptive width denotes dependence on the current forecast spread and is distinct from online adaptation of the calibration procedure.

Heat-pipe microreactors provide an application in which prescribed control motion produces coupled neutronic and thermal responses. Price \emph{et al.}\ investigated neural-surrogate-assisted optimization of microreactor reactivity-control configurations, while Tunkle \emph{et al.}\ developed reinforcement-learning control for microreactor transients and load following~\cite{price2022microreactor,tunkle2025microreactor}. These studies motivate forecasting responses to control-drum motion and examining the effects of sustained moves and holding periods. Here a reduced Modelica model simulated with Dymola couples six-group point kinetics to a lumped thermal network and balance-of-plant representation. A dynamic steam-generator metal node transfers heat to the secondary system, whose outlet temperature and quality are calculated from secondary heat addition. Operation-informed control trajectories prescribe selected maneuvers rather than represent a statistically validated population of actual reactor operations. They provide a controlled application test in which temporal input structure changes while the simulator and forecasting models remain fixed. This setting connects simulation-data design, recursive forecast accuracy, and interval calibration without assigning the study real-plant validation or closed-loop control performance.

\subsection{Objectives and contributions}
\label{sec:intro:contributions}

This study develops and evaluates MACE for multivariate autoregressive forecasting under prescribed control trajectories. Its objective is to determine how temporally structured simulation data support recursive prediction, whether ensemble disagreement provides a useful adaptive interval scale, and how the calibration distribution affects different coverage objectives. The framework uses ten LSTM members that advance independently through their own predicted histories. Their mean supplies the central forecast and their population-standard-deviation spread supplies the normalization scale. A common positive floor prevents vanishing denominators. Conformal calibration is performed using held-out trajectory samples after network training. The principal contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
 \item A formulation of stochastic simulation-data generation using temporally correlated control velocities and their integrated control trajectories. We relate the covariance, displacement dispersion, and initialization to the generated excitation and investigate the resulting dataset through its use in transient surrogate forecasting.
 \item An evaluation of multivariate autoregressive forecasting with a ten-member LSTM ensemble. Complete forecast trajectories are assessed separately from one-step model fitting, retaining individual member histories to examine prediction accuracy throughout control motion and the subsequent coupled response.
 \item The MACE formulation and comparison of ensemble-normalized conformal intervals under horizon-specific, per-output trajectory, and joint trajectory calibration. We distinguish their coverage events and compare interval coverage, width, and interval score against absolute-error conformal prediction using the same central forecasts and matched calibration objectives.
 \item A comparative analysis of calibration transfer and batch recalibration using operation-informed microreactor transients. Applying thresholds from two calibration distributions to the same independently simulated evaluation forecasts separates changes in uncertainty calibration from changes in forecasting accuracy. Maneuver and phase diagnostics supplement the population-level comparisons.
\end{enumerate}

These contributions build on established Gaussian-process sampling, recurrent forecasting, and normalized conformal prediction. The scientific focus is their specified construction and evaluation for control-conditioned multivariate transients, including the relationship between ensemble spread and forecast error under different calibration distributions. The application forecasts 15 output variables over 200~s using the reduced simulator as the reference. Forecasting accuracy and interval efficiency are evaluated alongside their appropriate coverage events, with whole-trajectory resampling used to quantify evaluation-sampling variability. This design assesses the usefulness and limitations of the combined framework without presupposing improved accuracy from recalibration or successful coverage transfer. The Methodology presents control-trajectory sampling, ensemble forecasting, and MACE calibration before describing the simulator and evaluation protocol. The Results assess recursive prediction and uncertainty under the stochastic distribution, followed by operation-informed forecasting and recalibration. The Discussion interprets the observed behavior in relation to the coverage assumptions and the scope of the reference model.
