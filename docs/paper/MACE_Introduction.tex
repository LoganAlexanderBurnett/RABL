\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Predicting the response of a physical system to a prescribed control trajectory is an important component of transient analysis, operating-condition assessment, and control development. In energy systems, changes in an actuator can produce coupled responses across several physical processes and time scales. A control action may first alter power or flow, while temperatures and other output variables continue to evolve after the actuator stops moving. Physics-based simulations provide a means of resolving these interactions, but repeated evaluation of candidate control trajectories motivates the development of surrogate models. For such models to be useful, they must represent the dynamics induced by control actions and provide an appropriate indication of the uncertainty in their forecasts.

Generating training data for this task requires preserving temporal dependencies. The physical state, $\mathbf{x}(t)$, depends on the preceding evolution of the system, so an admissible control value at one instant does not determine the resulting multivariate response trajectory. Its effect depends on when it is applied, how rapidly it changes, and the preceding control history. Accordingly, the relevant data unit is a trajectory sample comprising a control trajectory and its corresponding multivariate response trajectory. Sampling these objects requires specifying temporal excitation as well as the range of control values. The subsequent forecasting task must also preserve this structure: a model that predicts accurately from observed histories may behave differently when its own predictions become the history used to generate the remainder of a forecast trajectory.

These considerations connect three requirements for a useful surrogate: structured simulation data, accurate autoregressive prediction, and uncertainty bounds with a clearly defined coverage objective. Ensembles can provide a forecast-dependent measure of disagreement, while conformal prediction offers a framework for calibrating prediction intervals using held-out data. Their combination is particularly relevant when uncertainty varies along a transient. However, calibration depends on both the event being covered and the relationship between the calibration distribution and evaluation distribution. This study examines these requirements together, from stochastic control-trajectory generation to autoregressive ensemble forecasting and conformal calibration, with operation-informed microreactor transients providing the final application test.

\subsection{Literature review}
\label{sec:intro:literature}

The development of physical surrogates is closely tied to the selection of simulation data. For a nonlinear system, the training dataset determines which regions of the response space are represented and which relationships must be inferred beyond the available observations. Pestourie \emph{et al.}\ developed an active-learning approach for deep surrogates of partial differential equations, using a metasurface-design application to demonstrate the role of targeted simulation acquisition~\cite{pestourie2020surrogates}. Kapadia \emph{et al.}\ combined active data selection with surrogate modeling of parametric nonlinear systems, including parameter-specific reduced representations~\cite{kapadia2024surrogate}. Although their applications and acquisition procedures differ, these studies illustrate that data generation is part of the modeling problem. The choice of simulations cannot be separated from the behavior the surrogate is expected to predict.

For dynamical systems, this choice extends to the temporal structure of the applied control. Two control trajectories can span similar values while producing different responses because their rates, reversals, and dwell times differ. Experiment design therefore concerns the excitation of the system over time, rather than only the selection of isolated parameter values. Zimmer \emph{et al.}\ addressed this issue through safe active learning for time-series modeling with Gaussian processes, selecting temporally structured inputs while accounting for constraints~\cite{zimmer2018safe}. Their approach establishes a connection between trajectory selection, learning, and constrained exploration. A prespecified stochastic sampling procedure serves a different purpose: it produces a reproducible population of control trajectories before surrogate training, without selecting new simulations in response to model uncertainty.

Covariance-based sampling provides a mathematical way to describe such a population. A Gaussian process can encode correlations between control variations at different times, allowing the sampling procedure to distinguish slowly varying excitation from more rapid changes. The Mat\'ern covariance family supplies established choices for temporal correlation and regularity, with parameters that have been studied across statistics, numerical analysis, and machine learning~\cite{porcu2024matern}. Defining the process on control velocity and integrating it yields control trajectories whose variation is related to the velocity covariance. This construction makes temporal correlation and accumulated control dispersion explicit. In the original stochastic dataset, all control trajectories used for training, validation, calibration, and evaluation were sampled from the same velocity semivariogram. The retained trajectories satisfied a drum angular-velocity magnitude below $1^\circ\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$. This admissibility condition describes the accepted trajectories; it is not a hard limit imposed by the covariance itself. Initialization, discretization, and trajectory acceptance must therefore be considered alongside the variogram when interpreting the resulting sample distribution. The operation-informed maneuver families considered later are deliberately generated by a different, prescribed control procedure rather than drawn from the stochastic variogram.

Once simulation data are available, sequence models provide a means of learning their temporal relationships. Long short-term memory (LSTM) networks were introduced to address difficulties in learning dependencies over extended sequences~\cite{hochreiter1997lstm}. Their recurrent structure makes them relevant to physical time series, where the current response reflects earlier conditions. Nuclear applications include the neural forecasting of loss-of-coolant accident transients investigated by Radaideh \emph{et al.}~\cite{radaideh2020loca}. Appiah \emph{et al.}\ used LSTM system identification for sodium cold-trap purification-temperature control, connecting sequence prediction to a physical control problem~\cite{appiah2024coldtrap}. These studies establish useful application context for learned transient models, while their particular data, forecasting procedures, and control settings remain distinct from the prescribed-control task considered here.

Autoregressive deployment introduces an additional requirement beyond fitting observed sequences. During one-step training, the model receives a history drawn from the reference data. During recursive forecasting, predicted output variables are inserted into the next input history, and their errors can influence subsequent predictions. The resulting forecast trajectory must therefore be evaluated over the intended time domain. Low one-step error alone does not establish recursive accuracy. In a multivariate system, aggregate error can also conceal output variables whose dynamics are less accurately represented. This motivates evaluating complete forecast trajectories, retaining physical units for individual output variables, and examining performance during changing control and subsequent settling.

Ensembles offer a practical extension of point prediction by retaining multiple learned representations of the same system. Lakshminarayanan \emph{et al.}\ demonstrated deep ensembles as an approach to predictive uncertainty estimation using independently trained neural networks~\cite{lakshminarayanan2017deep}. Variation among members can reflect sensitivity to model fitting and available training data, producing disagreement that changes with the forecast conditions. For an autoregressive ensemble, member-specific predictions also produce different recursive histories. However, the spread of deterministic member forecasts is not automatically a predictive standard deviation with a known probability interpretation. Its value as an uncertainty scale must be assessed through the relationship between disagreement, observed forecast errors, and interval coverage.

This distinction becomes consequential when the evaluation distribution changes. Ovadia \emph{et al.}\ examined predictive uncertainty under dataset shift and showed the importance of evaluating uncertainty outside the conditions represented by the original assessment~\cite{ovadia2019shift}. Their principal benchmarks concern classification, so their results do not directly establish coverage properties for physical regression trajectories. They nevertheless motivate a separate examination of uncertainty transfer. An ensemble may retain an accurate mean forecast while the relationship between its spread and the residual errors changes. Conversely, increased disagreement does not by itself establish that a chosen interval will attain its intended coverage. Mean accuracy and uncertainty calibration must be assessed as separate properties.

Conformal prediction provides a framework for calibrating prediction sets around a fitted predictor using observed errors or other nonconformity measures. In split conformal regression, predictor fitting and interval calibration use separate data, and calibration scores determine an empirical threshold. Under exchangeability of the calibration and evaluation samples, an appropriate finite-sample order statistic supports a marginal coverage statement~\cite{lei2018regression,angelopoulos2023conformal}. The guarantee concerns a new sample from the relevant population; it is not automatically conditional on every operating condition or subset. This separation is useful for physical surrogates because a prediction model can remain fixed while the calibration stage determines how its errors are represented by intervals.

Adaptive interval widths are also an established part of conformal regression. Papadopoulos \emph{et al.}\ investigated normalized nonconformity measures that account for variation in prediction difficulty~\cite{papadopoulos2008normalized}. Normalization allows intervals to respond to a local scale while an empirical threshold supplies the calibration. Romano \emph{et al.}\ developed conformalized quantile regression, combining conditional-quantile estimates with conformal calibration~\cite{romano2019cqr}. These approaches use different predictors and scale constructions, but both illustrate that variable interval width and calibrated coverage can be pursued together. Using ensemble spread as the normalization scale is therefore a specific modeling choice within an existing statistical framework, rather than a new principle of conformal prediction.

Previous work has also combined ensembles with conformal inference for forecasting. Xu and Xie introduced the ensemble bootstrap prediction interval method, EnbPI, for dynamic time series~\cite{xu2021enbpi}. Their approach uses bootstrap ensembles and sequential residual information, with coverage analysis under assumptions appropriate to dependent time-series data. It provides a close precedent for combining ensemble prediction and empirical interval construction. The calibration setting considered here differs: each calibration observation is an entire trajectory sample, and dependence within that sample is retained when constructing its score. The statistical units and coverage events must therefore be distinguished from those of a sequential interval method applied along a single evolving series.

Multi-step forecasting further requires deciding which coordinates of a future response must be covered together. Stankevi\v{c}i\={u}t\.{e} \emph{et al.}\ developed conformal time-series forecasting, establishing sequence prediction as an existing setting for calibrated forecast regions~\cite{stankeviciute2021forecasting}. Sun and Yu addressed multivariate, multi-step forecasting through a copula-based conformal construction that accounts for dependence across forecast coordinates~\cite{sun2024copulacpts}. These studies show why a forecasting interval cannot be characterized adequately by a coverage percentage alone. Coverage at an individual horizon, coverage over an output's full forecast trajectory, and coverage of the complete multivariate response trajectory are different events. A comparison between methods should therefore identify both the score construction and the event to which its calibration applies.

In particular, separate calibration for each horizon--output pair does not automatically provide \emph{Horizon complete multivariate coverage}, which requires all output variables to be covered at a specified horizon. Likewise, separate calibration over time for each output does not establish \emph{Trajectory complete multivariate coverage}, which requires all output variables at every evaluated horizon to be covered simultaneously. Dependence among coordinates does not remove this distinction. A simultaneous objective must be represented in the calibration score, or otherwise addressed by a suitable joint construction. For physical systems with coupled outputs, defining these events explicitly is necessary to interpret what the reported uncertainty bounds support.

The relationship between distributions is equally important. A training distribution can differ from the population used for calibration and evaluation without invalidating ordinary split conformal inference, provided the predictor is fixed and the calibration and evaluation trajectory samples satisfy the required exchangeability. By contrast, retaining calibration from one population and evaluating on another generally removes that guarantee. Barber \emph{et al.}\ developed conformal methods beyond exchangeability, illustrating that such settings require additional treatment~\cite{barber2023exchangeability}. The existence of these methods does not make unchanged conformal intervals valid under arbitrary shift. For the present application, batch recalibration on independently generated trajectories from a new evaluation environment is a direct way to reassess the error scale, conditional on the existing surrogate remaining adequate for that environment.

Microreactor control provides a relevant setting in which to examine this distinction. Price \emph{et al.}\ investigated microreactor reactivity-control optimization using neural surrogates with evolutionary and swarm algorithms~\cite{price2022microreactor}. Tunkle \emph{et al.}\ studied transient and load-following drum control with deep reinforcement learning~\cite{tunkle2025microreactor}. These applications motivate forecasts conditioned on changing drum actions, but they do not validate the plant model or uncertainty bounds used here. Operation-informed control trajectories with finite-velocity maneuvers and holds can differ temporally from stochastic excitation even when their angle ranges overlap. They consequently provide a useful evaluation of both recursive prediction and the applicability of a calibration distribution, without requiring the present study to develop a control policy.

Collectively, the literature provides established methods for physical-surrogate data acquisition, sequence prediction, ensemble uncertainty, and conformal forecasting. The remaining task for this study is to connect these components through an explicit control-trajectory sampling procedure and an autoregressive deployment whose calibration units and coverage objectives are consistent. This requires evaluating whether ensemble disagreement supplies useful width adaptation, whether the selected objective is attained on independent evaluation trajectories, and how those conclusions change after recalibration. These questions motivate the formulation and evaluation described next.

\subsection{Objectives and contributions}
\label{sec:intro:contributions}

This work develops and evaluates MACE, an ensemble-normalized conformal approach for control-conditioned autoregressive forecasting. The workflow begins with temporally correlated velocity sampling and integration to generate control trajectories for a physics simulator, retaining profiles that meet the prescribed angular-velocity and angle requirements. A ten-member LSTM ensemble is trained on the resulting trajectory data and recursively predicts the output variables under prescribed future control. MACE then uses the ensemble mean as the forecast and ensemble spread, supplemented by a common positive additive floor in output-scaled coordinates, as the scale for normalized absolute errors. The floor maintains a positive denominator when member disagreement is small. Conformal calibration selects thresholds from held-out trajectory samples; it does not fit a new forecast model.

We distinguish two per-output constructions and an additional joint calibration. \emph{MACE-Horizon} calibrates normalized errors separately for each horizon--output pair. Its objective is marginal coverage for each specified pair. \emph{MACE-Trajectory} first computes each calibration sample's maximum normalized error over time for an output variable and then selects that output's conformal quantile. Its objective is coverage over the complete evaluated trajectory of that individual output. Joint trajectory calibration instead takes the maximum normalized error over both time and output variables within each calibration sample, targeting Trajectory complete multivariate coverage. Horizon complete multivariate coverage is also reported as a diagnostic, with its joint event kept distinct from ordinary MACE-Horizon calibration. All trajectory objectives refer to the evaluated time grid.

The width adaptation in MACE arises from forecast-dependent ensemble spread, while calibration determines the multiplier appropriate to the selected event. A common floor can reduce this adaptation when spread is very small, as illustrated briefly by steam quality in the application. Increasing the floor need not increase every interval width, because recalibration also changes the conformal quantile. The formulation is evaluated against absolute-error conformal prediction with matching calibration data and coverage objectives. Coverage, full width, width ratios, and interval scores are considered together so that efficiency is not inferred from narrow intervals that fail their intended objective.

We then examine calibration transfer and recalibration while holding the trained models and training scaler fixed. The same operation-informed evaluation forecasts are assessed using calibration from the original stochastic trajectories and calibration from a new, independently generated operation-informed set. The first arm is a distribution-transfer diagnostic; its original nominal objective is not assumed to remain valid under the changed evaluation distribution. The second arm uses calibration and evaluation trajectories drawn independently from the same prespecified population. Recalibration changes the interval thresholds and bounds, but not the ensemble mean. Its usefulness therefore depends on demonstrating adequate forecast accuracy separately from the uncertainty assessment.

The demonstration uses a reduced microreactor model implemented in Modelica and simulated in Dymola over a 0--200~s domain. The model couples point kinetics, a lumped reactor thermal network, and a dynamic steam-generator metal node. Outlet temperature and quality are calculated from secondary-side heat addition; they are not fixed outputs. The analysis concerns surrogate error relative to this simulator under prescribed control trajectories. It provides an offline forecasting evaluation rather than experimental plant validation or a demonstration of closed-loop control. Accuracy and uncertainty are assessed over complete trajectories and separately during motion, early settling, and late holds, with paired whole-trajectory bootstrap comparisons preserving the common evaluation samples.

Accordingly, the four contributions of this study are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item A specified stochastic control-trajectory sampling procedure that uses a common velocity semivariogram, integrates the resulting correlated velocities into control trajectories, and retains admissible profiles with drum angular-velocity magnitude below $1^\circ\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$.
    \item A ten-member LSTM surrogate ensemble trained on trajectory-disjoint data and evaluated through member-specific autoregressive forecasting, including the coordinate mapping required to return output predictions to the input history.
    \item A MACE formulation that combines ensemble-dependent scaling with conformal calibration and distinguishes horizon--output, per-output trajectory, and complete multivariate trajectory coverage objectives.
    \item A controlled comparison of original and operation-informed calibration distributions using fixed models, a fixed training scaler, common evaluation forecasts, and paired assessments of accuracy, coverage, width, and interval score across maneuver phases.
\end{enumerate}

The methodology section presents the sampling construction, ensemble forecasting, MACE calibration, plant model, and evaluation protocol. The results first assess autoregressive accuracy and uncertainty on the stochastic evaluation distribution, then examine operation-informed forecasts and recalibration. The discussion addresses the implications of adaptive scaling, coverage assumptions, and application limits, followed by the conclusions.
